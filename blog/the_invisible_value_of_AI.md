# The Invisible Value of AI

*October 25, 2026*

## The value nobody talks about

When people talk about what AI brings to the table, they almost always mean automation. Tasks done faster, cheaper processes, jobs being reshaped. In other words, value as seen from the company's side.

What I'm interested in here is a different kind of value, one we hear much less about: what hundreds of millions of people get every day out of a simple conversation with ChatGPT or another assistant. Understanding something, learning, writing, or just having some help thinking things through.

To get a sense of how big that is, I asked myself a simple question: how much would you have to pay me to stop using AI for a month? My answer surprised me a bit: around €200. To be fair, that includes my work use, and I use it a lot for work. But a good chunk of that €200 is for everything else: thinking, writing, learning, sorting out the small problems of everyday life. That's the part people rarely talk about, and that's the part I want to dig into.

## What would it take to give it up?

I know I'm a heavy user, way above average. But it turns out researchers have asked the exact same question, at scale. Erik Brynjolfsson's team at Stanford asked representative samples of Americans how much they'd need to be paid to give up AI chatbots for a month. The average answer went from $98 in July 2025 to $124.50 in March 2026. Multiply that by the 115 million adults who use them and you get $172 billion a year, in the US alone.

What struck me most, statistician that I am, is the gap between the mean and the median. The median is only $11.40, ten times lower. In other words, most people put a fairly modest value on AI, and a minority of heavy users pulls the average up. With my €200, I'm clearly one of them. But the median more than tripled in a year, which tells you that the more people learn to use these tools, the more they get out of them.

If you extrapolate to the whole world, with close to a billion weekly users for ChatGPT alone, you probably end up with several hundred billion dollars a year, maybe more. And here's the interesting bit: almost all of that value goes to users. In the US, in 2024, it was estimated at more than ten times what the companies selling these tools were earning. Which isn't that surprising when you consider that only about 5% of ChatGPT users pay for a subscription.

## Where the value actually comes from

So if it's not automation, where does all this value come from? For me, it mostly comes down to one thing: AI gives people access to help that, for most of them, simply didn't exist before. I see a few areas where that's especially true.

The first is decision-making. When you're facing a tough choice, AI acts a bit like a friend who helps you think it through. It reframes the problem, asks the questions you'd been avoiding, suggests options you hadn't considered. It doesn't decide for you, but to some extent, it helps you decide better.

Then there's communication. We've all been in a tense or awkward situation without quite knowing how to handle it. Finding the right words can take a huge amount of time and energy. And honestly, I find AI really helpful here. It helps you step back, rephrase things without sounding aggressive, and sometimes defuse a conflict before it blows up.

Learning, obviously. Recently I had to get up to speed on a skill to prepare for an interview, and I think I learned about twice as fast as I would have on my own. Why? Partly because you get the information you need right away, without digging for it. But mostly because AI does the sorting for you. It's a bit like a good math teacher: when they go over an exercise, they don't just hand you the solution, they point out the key concepts, the ones that actually unlock the problem, and take the time to explain them. Not every teacher does that, but AI does it pretty well. On top of that, it let me rehearse the interview itself, follow-up questions included, until I felt ready. No book can do that.

For young people, it's also personalized homework help, available at any hour, with no judgment. It can also be someone to talk to about what to study or which path to take, which matters a lot when you consider that not all parents have the know-how or the network to guide their kids.

Finally, there's mental health. AI can play a role fairly close to psychological support or personal coaching: putting words on anxiety, spotting a pattern that keeps repeating, getting some distance from a rough patch. Obviously it doesn't replace a therapist, and this is probably the most debatable item on the list. But therapy is expensive and waiting lists are often long. For a lot of people, having someone available to work on themselves with is genuinely valuable.

And none of this is just for young people. We tend to associate learning and self-improvement with school, but adults have plenty to work on too: making better decisions, communicating better, picking up new skills. And most of them have never had this kind of support.

What strikes me is that in none of these cases does AI replace a service people used to buy. Nobody was paying a tutor at 11 p.m., or a lawyer to look over their lease. That help wasn't too expensive. It just wasn't available.

## A building block

The reason this value is so broad is that ChatGPT isn't a specialized tool. It's not an education app, or a health service, or a writing tool. It's more like a building block that everyone builds their own uses on.

It reminds me of electricity. Economists call this kind of innovation a "general-purpose technology": something that doesn't do one thing, but unlocks thousands. Electricity didn't just light up homes. It made the fridge, the subway, radio and the modern hospital possible, along with plenty of things nobody imagined when it was first invented.

I think generative AI is the same kind of shift. We're only at the beginning, and most of the uses that will really matter in ten years probably don't exist yet.

## The flip side

Of course, all of this comes with trade-offs, and they're not trivial.

The first is the feeling of being outmatched. I recently did a technical interview on generative AI, and the interviewer was an AI. It didn't let anything slide: tough questions, sharp follow-ups on every answer, and it kept digging into the spots where I was weakest, to see if there was real substance behind what I was saying. I'm not talking about general superiority, but for this specific exercise, I think AI is intellectually superior to a human recruiter. Plus, it never gets tired and is never held back by awkwardness or politeness. It's not a fair fight.

There's also a kind of dehumanization. Lately I've been messaging a lot on LinkedIn while looking for freelance work, and I increasingly had the feeling that one AI was talking to another AI, with a human on each end copy-pasting the messages. And yet our imperfections are part of what makes us human.

There's isolation. Time spent with AI is time not spent with other people. The friend you would have called to think through a problem, you don't call anymore; you ask the AI. What helps us think or feel better can also, little by little, pull us away from others.

And then there's sycophancy. AI tends to agree with you, and I've noticed it myself. But a friend who helps you think is valuable precisely because they can push back. If you're constantly validated, you risk becoming a bit more self-centered. There are guardrails, of course: an AI won't encourage you to go punch your neighbor, even if you explain you have excellent reasons. But for everything short of that, the bias toward telling you you're right is very real.

So the value of AI is anything but automatic. It all depends on how you use it: as support to grow, or as a substitute for effort and for real relationships.

## Wrapping up

We tend to judge AI by what it can automate. I think that misses the point. Right now, its main value isn't in factories or in finance departments' spreadsheets. It's in the lives of hundreds of millions of people who now have access, at any hour and almost for free, to something that helps them think, learn, or find the right words.

So, how much would you have to be paid to give it up?
